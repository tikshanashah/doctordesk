# 02. WHAT IS THE DOCTOR'S DESK?

The Doctor's Desk is a **doctor-first editorial and professional utility platform**.

It sits at the intersection of:

- editorial content
- curated information
- practical tools
- professional documentation
- medico-legal intelligence
- healthcare developments
- medical culture
- useful templates
- discovery

It is not simply a blog.

It is not simply a news portal.

It is not simply a legal resource.

It is not a CME platform.

It is not a CoverYou insurance content page.

It is a **curated professional internet layer for doctors**.

The long-term ambition is:

> **To build a place where doctors can come to discover useful things, understand complicated things, use practical things and keep things worth coming back to.**

---

# 03. THE BIG IDEA

Doctors already have access to enormous amounts of information.

The problem is not lack of information.

The problem is:

- fragmentation
- noise
- lack of curation
- lack of context
- lack of time
- poor discoverability
- information buried inside PDFs
- information spread across multiple platforms
- information that may be technically relevant but practically useless
- useful things that doctors do not know exist

The Doctor's Desk exists to solve the **finding and filtering problem**.

The fundamental promise is:

> **We have done some of the finding for you.**

The platform should help a doctor discover something and think:

> "I didn't know I needed this."

or:

> "I had been looking for exactly this."

or:

> "This is actually useful."

# 05. HOW THE AUDIENCE SHOULD BE TREATED

This is a critical principle.

Doctors should be treated as:

- intelligent
- busy
- experienced
- professionally responsible
- capable of understanding nuance
- uninterested in being lectured

The writing should not sound like:

> "Let us teach you what informed consent is."

Instead it should sound like:

> "Here is a case, question, pattern, tool or idea that may be worth having on your desk."

The platform should respect the reader's intelligence.

# 08. WHAT THE DOCTOR'S DESK IS NOT

Do not allow the project to drift into any of the following.

## NOT A GENERIC BLOG

Avoid:

> "5 tips for doctors to improve their practice"

unless the topic is genuinely useful and has an original angle.

## NOT A MEDICAL NEWS COPY MACHINE

Do not republish headlines from the internet.

"Current" should mean:

> What is changing and why might a doctor care?

not:

> What happened today?

## NOT A LEGAL PORTAL

The Desk can include medico-legal content, but it should not look like a law firm website.

Avoid overwhelming users with:

- statutes
- sections
- legal jargon
- fear messaging
- case dumps

## NOT A CME PLATFORM

The Desk can contain educational content, but its editorial layer should not become academic lecture notes.

## NOT A FEAR-BASED INSURANCE PROPERTY

Never reduce the platform to:

> "Here are things that can get doctors sued."

CoverYou should have credibility in the background without turning every interaction into a risk message.

## NOT A CORPORATE MARKETING BLOG

The platform should not feel like every article eventually leads to:

> "Buy insurance from CoverYou."

Trust should come first.

## NOT AN AI CONTENT FARM

The site should never feel like hundreds of generic AI-generated articles.

Every piece needs an editorial reason to exist.

A story can involve:

- a person
- an event
- an invention
- a patient
- a professional dilemma
- an ethical change
- a scientific discovery
- a cultural shift
- a medical institution
- a forgotten idea

The key question:

> **Is there a story here worth knowing?**

# 18. FROM THE ARCHIVE — FORMAT

Preferred format:

- 250–310 words
- approximately 1–1.5 minute read
- strong opening hook
- narrative flow
- curiosity-led
- simple but intelligent language
- one central idea
- useful historical context
- closing reflection or "thought worth keeping"
- references
- image credit / rights note where relevant

# 33. IMPORTANT CHANGE TO THE CONSENT TOOL

The original HTML demo includes wording suggesting that the generated checklist could be attached to the standard consent form "to prove a tailored discussion took place."

That wording should **not automatically be preserved as final product copy**.

The mature positioning should be:

> The checklist structures and documents the conversation.

It should NOT promise:

- legal protection
- immunity from litigation
- guaranteed defensibility
- proof of compliance
- guaranteed reduction in liability

# 51. CASE INTELLIGENCE — WHY IT EXISTS

Doctors often encounter professional situations that are too nuanced to reduce to:

- "legal advice"
- "don't do this"
- "always do that"

Cases help show:

- what happened
- what was alleged
- what facts mattered
- what evidence mattered
- what courts actually examined
- what courts actually held

The value is reasoning.

---

# 52. CASE INTELLIGENCE — CORE EDITORIAL IDEA

A potential editorial philosophy:

> **Cases where medicine gets complicated enough for the law to have an opinion.**

This is an editorial framing, not necessarily a public tagline.

---

Do not simply publish hundreds of judgments.

- practical relevance

- interesting reasoning
- recurring doctor situations
- documentation

- emergencies

# 54. CASE SELECTION RULE

# 56. CASE INTELLIGENCE — REQUIRED STRUCTURE

Each case should use:

## CASE ON THE DESK
An anonymised scenario.

## THE QUESTION
What professional/legal question does the case raise?

## WHAT DID THE COURT LOOK AT?
Focus on:
- records
- evidence
- expert opinion
- chronology
- medical reasoning
- communication
- causation
- documentation

## WHAT DID THE COURT SAY?
State the actual judicial holding carefully.

## DESK NOTE
A practical takeaway.

---

# 57. CASE INTELLIGENCE — CRITICAL DISTINCTION

Always distinguish:

> **Allegation ≠ fact ≠ finding**

An allegation should never be written as if established.

A doctor's defence should not be written as if automatically accepted.

A court holding must be represented accurately.

Prefer:

> "Negligence was not established."

or:

> "The court did not find the evidence sufficient to establish negligence."

depending on the exact judgment.

# 72. THE WRITER'S POSITION

The writer should sound like:

> **A good magazine writer who understands doctors.**

The writer is NOT:

- a social media copywriter
- a lawyer frightening doctors
- an AI summarising Wikipedia

---

# 73. PREFERRED NARRATIVE BEHAVIOUR

Start with:

- a scene
- a surprising observation
- a question
- a contrast
- a familiar thing made strange

Then introduce the information.

Avoid generic beginnings such as:

> "Throughout history..."

or:

> "In today's rapidly changing world..."

---

# 74. GOOD OPENING STYLE

Reference rhythm:

> Imagine trying to listen to a heart without a stethoscope.

Then:

> No familiar lub-dub through rubber tubing. No instrument around your neck. Just your ear, the patient, and whatever you could hear.

Then:

> In 1816, a French physician tried something unexpectedly simple.

This is the desired editorial rhythm.

---

# 75. AVOID AI-ISH PHRASES

Avoid repeated structures such as:

- "The interesting part is..."
- "The important shift was..."
- "This shows that..."
- "For doctors, this means..."
- "At the heart of..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "It's not just X. It's Y."
- "What this tells us is..."
- "The takeaway is simple..."

Use natural language instead.

---

# 76. ENDINGS

Do not automatically conclude with:

> "And that's why..."

or:

> "A reminder that..."

or:

> "For doctors, this is an important lesson..."

Let the story land naturally.

A brief closing thought can work.

Example:

> Sometimes, a breakthrough begins with nothing more than a better way of listening.

# 157. WORKING INSTRUCTIONS FOR CLAUDE

When working on The Doctor's Desk, always follow these principles:

## THINK BEFORE WRITING
Determine the purpose, audience, editorial angle and user value first.

## PROTECT THE VOICE
Do not make copy more generic merely to make it sound "professional."

## TREAT DOCTORS AS INTELLIGENT
Do not explain obvious concepts unnecessarily.

## PREFER ORIGINAL ANGLES
Avoid generic healthcare topics unless the framing makes them distinctive.

## PRIORITISE USEFULNESS
Every tool and practical piece should solve something real.

## VERIFY SENSITIVE INFORMATION
Medical, legal and current claims require source verification.

## USE PRIMARY SOURCES
Whenever possible, work backwards from the original source.

## DISTINGUISH CLAIMS FROM FINDINGS
Especially in medico-legal content.

## DON'T INVENT
If a source does not support a claim, do not add it merely because it sounds plausible.

## CONNECT CONTENT
Look for meaningful links between:
- article
- tool
- case
- template
- guidance

## KEEP THE EXPERIENCE EDITORIAL
Do not turn the Desk into a SaaS dashboard.

## DESIGN FOR RETURN
A useful platform is one people come back to.

## BUILD THE SYSTEM, NOT JUST THE PAGE
The content database, taxonomy, search and relationships matter as much as the homepage.
